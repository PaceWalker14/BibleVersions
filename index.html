01 / 07 · Formal equivalence

NASB

New American Standard Bible

The word-for-word workhorse of serious Bible study.

First published
1971 (NT 1963)
Revisions
1995, 2020
Publisher
The Lockman Foundation
Reading level
Grade 11
Old Testament source
Masoretic Text (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia)
New Testament source
Critical Greek text (Nestle-Aland)

Translation approach

Word-for-wordThought-for-thoughtParaphrase

Follows the Hebrew and Greek as closely as English grammar allows — word order, verb tenses and repeated words are kept wherever possible, and words the translators added for sense are set in italics.

John 3:16

For God so loved the world, that He gave His only Son, so that everyone who believes in Him will not perish, but have eternal life.

Advantages

  • Very precise — ideal for word studies and comparing with the original languages
  • Consistent: the same Greek or Hebrew word is usually rendered by the same English word
  • Italics show exactly which words the translators supplied
  • Capitalises pronouns that refer to God, which helps in tricky passages

Disadvantages

  • Wooden, choppy English in places — hard to read aloud
  • Literal idioms can obscure the meaning for newer readers
  • Less suited to reading long sections in one sitting

Best for

  • Deep study
  • Word studies
  • Sermon prep

Did you know?

The NASB began as an update of the 1901 American Standard Version, which was itself a revision of the King James Version.

02 / 07 · Essentially literal

ESV

English Standard Version

Essentially literal, with a literary ear.

First published
2001
Revisions
2007, 2011, 2016
Publisher
Crossway
Reading level
Grade 10
Old Testament source
Masoretic Text (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia)
New Testament source
Critical Greek text (UBS / Nestle-Aland)

Translation approach

Word-for-wordThought-for-thoughtParaphrase

Aims to be “as literal as possible” while keeping natural, dignified English. Built on the 1971 Revised Standard Version and revised by a team of more than 100 people.

John 3:16

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.

Advantages

  • A strong balance of accuracy and readability
  • Elegant, traditional phrasing that works well for memorisation and public reading
  • Huge range of editions and study tools, including the ESV Study Bible
  • Keeps key theological terms like “propitiation” and “justification”

Disadvantages

  • Keeps some awkward or old-fashioned word order inherited from the RSV
  • Less natural for children and new readers than the NIV or NLT
  • Often keeps masculine wording (“brothers”, “man”) and moves inclusive meanings to footnotes

Best for

  • Study & teaching
  • Memorisation
  • Church reading

Did you know?

The ESV’s family tree runs back through the RSV (1952), the American Standard Version (1901) and the English Revised Version (1885) to the King James Version.

03 / 07 · Formal equivalence

KJV

King James Version

The 1611 classic that shaped the English language.

First published
1611
Revisions
1769 (the standard text used today)
Commissioned by
King James I of England
Reading level
Grade 12
Old Testament source
Masoretic Text (Bomberg Rabbinic Bible)
New Testament source
Textus Receptus

Translation approach

Word-for-wordThought-for-thoughtParaphrase

A word-for-word translation made by about 47 scholars working in six companies at Westminster, Oxford and Cambridge, building heavily on earlier English Bibles such as Tyndale’s.

John 3:16

For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son, that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life.

Advantages

  • Majestic, rhythmic language that is memorable and beautiful read aloud
  • Enormous historical and cultural influence — quoted in literature, music and everyday speech
  • Public domain (outside the UK), so it is free to copy and share
  • The wording many people already know by heart

Disadvantages

  • Archaic English (“thee”, “thou”, “-eth”) can be hard to follow
  • Some words have changed meaning — “let” once meant “hinder”, “conversation” meant “conduct”
  • Based on later Greek manuscripts; a few verses (e.g. 1 John 5:7–8) contain words missing from the earliest copies

Best for

  • Literary reading
  • Memorisation
  • Traditional worship

Did you know?

Linguist David Crystal counted 257 everyday English idioms that come from it — from “the powers that be” to “by the skin of your teeth”.

04 / 07 · Formal equivalence

NKJV

New King James Version

The King James tradition in modern English.

First published
1982 (NT 1979)
Publisher
Thomas Nelson
Reading level
Grade 9
Old Testament source
Masoretic Text (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia)
New Testament source
Textus Receptus, with footnotes for other manuscript traditions

Translation approach

Word-for-wordThought-for-thoughtParaphrase

Updates the KJV’s vocabulary and grammar while keeping its sentence structure and textual base. Footnotes flag where the modern critical (NU) and Majority (M) Greek texts differ.

John 3:16

For God so loved the world that He gave His only begotten Son, that whoever believes in Him should not perish but have everlasting life.

Advantages

  • The familiar KJV cadence without “thee” and “thou”
  • Literal and dependable for study
  • Footnotes show textual differences openly
  • A natural bridge for readers who grew up with the KJV

Disadvantages

  • Sentence structure can still feel formal and old-fashioned
  • Uses the Textus Receptus rather than the earliest manuscripts for its main text
  • Harder for children and new believers than dynamic translations

Best for

  • KJV lovers
  • Study
  • Preaching

Did you know?

More than 130 scholars, church leaders and lay Christians worked on it for seven years — and unlike the 1611 KJV, it capitalises pronouns that refer to God.

05 / 07 · Optimal equivalence

CSB

Christian Standard Bible

Optimal equivalence: faithful and highly readable.

First published
2017
Based on
The HCSB (2004)
Publisher
Holman Bible Publishers
Reading level
Grade 7
Old Testament source
Masoretic Text (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia)
New Testament source
Critical Greek text (Nestle-Aland 28 / UBS 5)

Translation approach

Word-for-wordThought-for-thoughtParaphrase

Uses “optimal equivalence”: a literal rendering whenever it reads clearly in English, and a more dynamic one only when a literal phrase would hide the meaning.

John 3:16

For God loved the world in this way: He gave his one and only Son, so that everyone who believes in him will not perish but have eternal life.

Advantages

  • Excellent balance — easy to read yet faithful to the original
  • Clear, modern English that reads aloud well
  • Keeps key theological terms while smoothing awkward idioms
  • Translated by scholars from many denominations

Disadvantages

  • Newer, so fewer commentaries and study resources are keyed to it
  • Occasional freer renderings can frustrate detailed word study
  • Wording is less familiar when reading alongside older translations

Best for

  • Everyday reading
  • Small groups
  • Teens & new readers

Did you know?

Its predecessor, the HCSB, often printed God’s name as “Yahweh”; the CSB switched almost all of those back to “the LORD”.

06 / 07 · Balanced (word & thought)

NIV

New International Version

The balanced, best-selling modern translation.

First published
1978 (NT 1973)
Revisions
1984, 2011
Publisher
Biblica · Zondervan
Reading level
Grade 7–8
Old Testament source
Masoretic Text (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia), with other ancient sources
New Testament source
Eclectic critical Greek text

Translation approach

Word-for-wordThought-for-thoughtParaphrase

Balances word-for-word accuracy with thought-for-thought clarity, aiming for natural contemporary English. It is kept up to date by the independent Committee on Bible Translation.

John 3:16

For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life.

Advantages

  • Very readable while staying faithful to the original meaning
  • One of the most widely used translations — a huge range of editions, apps and resources
  • Great for public reading, small groups and new believers
  • Regularly updated to reflect scholarship and changes in English

Disadvantages

  • Interprets more than literal translations, so it is less precise for word study
  • The 2011 update’s gender-inclusive choices are still debated
  • Smooths over some repeated words and wordplay in the original

Best for

  • Everyday reading
  • Church & groups
  • New believers

Did you know?

Its original team of over 100 scholars came from several English-speaking countries and many denominations — the “International” in the name.

07 / 07 · Dynamic equivalence

NLT

New Living Translation

Thought-for-thought clarity that reads like today.

First published
1996
Revisions
2004, 2007, 2015
Publisher
Tyndale House
Reading level
Grade 6
Old Testament source
Masoretic Text (Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia)
New Testament source
Critical Greek text (UBS / Nestle-Aland)

Translation approach

Word-for-wordThought-for-thoughtParaphrase

Translates whole thoughts rather than individual words, asking how the original author would say it in natural English today. Produced by 90 scholars from many denominations.

John 3:16

For this is how God loved the world: He gave his one and only Son, so that everyone who believes in him will not perish but have eternal life.

Advantages

  • Extremely clear and easy to read — great for long stretches
  • Ideal for new believers, teens and people reading English as a second language
  • Explains idioms and ancient customs in plain words
  • Engaging for devotional and out-loud reading

Disadvantages

  • More interpretation is built into the text itself
  • Loses some repeated key words, imagery and wordplay
  • Not ideal on its own for detailed word or doctrinal study

Best for

  • Devotional reading
  • New believers
  • Reading the whole Bible

Did you know?

It started as a revision of Kenneth Taylor’s 1971 paraphrase, The Living Bible, but grew into a fresh translation from the Hebrew and Greek.
